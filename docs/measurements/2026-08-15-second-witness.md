# A second witness to the significator series, and what it disagreed about

**2026-08-15.** Every rule in `out/textual/significator-series-rules.jsonl` rested on one
copy, one translation, one translator. A second copy was acquired and resolved against the
same rules. ⭐ The headline is not that it agreed. It agreed four times out of five, and the
fifth disagreement is the one a consumer's arithmetic actually depends on.

---

## 1. What the second copy is, and why not one of the obvious ones

The recorded refusal said what would close it: *a second translation of the same work,
acquired and resolved*. Four candidates were surveyed and three were rejected, for reasons
worth keeping because each is a different failure.

| Candidate | Rejected because |
|---|---|
| Two independent English translations | Both in copyright and sold commercially; no retrievable pinned copy. ⚠ Obtaining one is a purchase, not a fetch |
| An English copy on a public archive, uploader named as creator | ⛔ **No translator identifiable anywhere in the item.** A copy whose translator cannot be named is not a second *witness* — it could be a re-typing of the first, and nothing in it would show that |
| A fuller printing of the **same** translator | ⛔ Not a second witness at all. It would close the *extent* refusal and control the held part-copy, and it cannot corroborate a translation against itself |
| ⭐ **A Hindi translation with commentary, carrying the sutras in their own script** | **Taken.** A different translator, a different language, and the original alongside |

⚠ **A search by title returns a different work by the same author name** — the two top
results were the *Purva Mimamsa* Sutras, a work of philosophy. The copy taken was checked for
which work it is before anything else: the vocabulary of the astrological work runs to
hundreds of occurrences in it and the philosophical work's runs to single figures.

## 2. What it settled

| Rule | Verdict |
|---|---|
| the body highest in degrees heads the series, over seven bodies or eight | ✅ corroborated |
| the body next in degrees takes the second place | ✅ corroborated |
| the body next again takes the third | ✅ corroborated |
| a tie merges two places and the node fills the vacancy | ✅ corroborated |
| **the node is ranked by reversed degrees** | ⛔ **forked** |

⭐ Where they agree, **two translators working in two languages agree** — which no second
printing of one translation could have established. The first rule is stated almost sentence
for sentence in both, including the part a consumer is most likely to narrow: both offer the
seven-body and the eight-body reckoning, and neither chooses.

⭐⭐ **Two of the corroborated rules are the first copy's translator's *notes*, and a second
edition was expected to be unable to speak to them at all.** It speaks to both. ⚠ What that
establishes is narrower than it looks, and the fixture says so: it establishes that the rule
is **attested by two commentators independently** rather than being one translator's gloss.
⛔ It does **not** establish that the first translator's note says what we recorded — only
another copy of *that* translation can witness that, and the refusal was rewritten to that
narrower subject rather than deleted.

## 3. The fork, which is about scope rather than content

⛔ **Both copies contain the rule that the ascending node's degrees are read backwards. They
attach it to different sutras, governing different determinations.**

* The first copy states it in its notes to the sutra **founding the series**, so it governs
  how the node is ranked when the series is built.
* The second states it at a later sutra governing a **different and narrower** determination
  — and states it as arithmetic rather than as description, which is *more* specific, not
  less. At the founding sutra it instead gives a different reason for leaving the descending
  node out: that its degrees always equal the ascending node's.

⭐ **A disagreement about a rule's scope moves more charts than a disagreement about its
content.** A consumer that reverses the node's degrees when ranking the series is following
one copy against the other, and the node's rank changes with the reading — so the series
does, for any chart in which the node would place at all.

⛔ The fork is recorded and not resolved. Which copy is right is not a recorder's question,
and both readings are located.

## 4. ⭐⭐ Presence of a script is not fidelity of a script

The second copy carries the original's script in quantity — some 335 000 code points, about
73% of the rendering. ⛔ So the control that stood for *"no primary text is reachable here"*
answers **yes** for it, and answering yes is exactly how it would be misread.

⚠ **The same machine reading damaged the sutra lines while capturing the commentary around
them cleanly.** The sutra the whole series hangs on is rendered with several letters wrong.

⭐ **The check is internal, and it had to be.** Asking whether a scanned sutra matches the
form the work is *conventionally* transmitted in would need an authority this repository does
not hold, and supplying one from the recorder's own memory is precisely the unsourced claim
the locus discipline exists to refuse. So the question asked is one the copy answers about
itself: **its own commentary names a word as occurring in that sutra, and the sutra as
rendered does not contain it.**

⇒ A new refusal reason, `script_present_but_passage_not_faithful`, distinct from the existing
`script_not_present_in_this_rendering` on purpose. ⛔ A repository owning only the second
would have read a true presence result as licence to cite the original.

⚠ The control holds only when **both** are true — script present *and* passage unfaithful —
so it fails if the script ever goes missing and equally if the passage ever becomes faithful.
Either would mean the refusal built on it had stopped describing the copy.

## 5. ⛔⛔ The extent was a property of the regular expression, not of the book

This is the finding most worth carrying, because the first measurement looked like a result.

Three detectors were run over the second copy's own pada-closing markers. The copy prints the
same closing sentence at every boundary, and the machine reading damaged it **differently at
every occurrence**:

| Detector keyed on | Divisions found |
|---|---|
| the author's name | 9 |
| the word for a division | 10 |
| the closing verb | 12 |
| ⭐ **the union of all three** | **14** |

Each detector missed boundaries the others found, and every miss was a different corruption
of one sentence — a letter substituted in the author's name, a word split across a space, an
inflection dropped.

⛔ **Published on the first measurement, this file would have said "six divisions not found"
as a fact about the printed book, when it was a fact about a regular expression.** ⭐ *A
walker that knows one carrying form has measured the wrong subject.*

Two further consequences were taken rather than noted:

* **Every marker spelling is now read off the copy.** Seven had been written by guessing the
  regular form; six of those matched nothing, and the one that matched was right by luck. ⚠ A
  spelling that is guessed and never counted is the defect the whole discipline exists to
  stop, and it appeared here in the module enforcing it.
* ⛔ **No absence may be measured over this copy.** An absence is only as wide as the extent
  it is taken over; this extent is a **lower bound** rather than a measurement, and an absence
  over a lower bound reports the recorder's alphabet as the book's silence. That is a named
  refusal, `extent_of_the_copy_is_a_lower_bound`, not a caveat in prose.

⚠ **One closing marker is printed and deliberately unlisted.** The digitiser's page footer
landed inside it and destroyed the words naming which pada it ends. Its position between two
located neighbours would settle it — ⛔ and position is not what a boundary marker is for. A
boundary that cannot say what it bounds establishes nothing, and inferring its identity would
be the recorder deciding rather than reading.

⚠ Worth knowing generally: that digitiser's footer is stamped into the text layer some 176
times, and one of them landed **between two sutras**. A fragment quoted across a page break in
this copy can contain material that is not part of the work at all.

## 6. The refusing direction

⭐ Five corroboration rows were written and every locus reported exactly one occurrence. ⛔
**That is the accepting run, and a walker that never checked would look identical.** So the
path was driven off its accepting value, each defect built by splitting the real fragment and
asserting the text changed first:

| Defect | Outcome |
|---|---|
| the real table, unmutated (null control) | ✅ accepted, 5 rows |
| a word inserted into a real fragment | ✅ refused — occurs 0 times |
| a fragment shortened until it occurs 286 times | ✅ refused — locates nothing |
| the second passage of a two-passage rule made unresolvable | ✅ refused |
| a real fragment resolved against the **other** copy | ✅ refused — occurs 0 times |

**5/5.** ⚠ The last is the one worth keeping: it is what shows the resolution is against the
copy it names rather than against whichever text is to hand.

## 7. What this does not settle

* ⛔ The **first copy's notes as that translator's own words** still rest on one copy. Only a
  second printing of that translation can witness them.
* ⛔ The sutras **as either copy prints them** are still not citable — the first rendering
  carries no Sanskrit at all, the second carries it damaged.
* ⛔ The **absence** recorded in the fixture is still measured over one copy, and is not
  extended.
* ⚠ The fork is **recorded, not resolved**, and resolving it would need a third copy or the
  original in a faithful rendering.
